# Sustainability Strategy: BOSC Community Library

**Document:** SUSTAINABILITY.md  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Date:** May 2026  

---

## Executive Summary

Long-term sustainability is the most critical — and most frequently neglected — dimension of open-source project governance. The BOSC Community Library is not merely a student project; it is a public good. This document outlines two primary funding models considered for sustaining the library and makes a concrete recommendation grounded in the library's public-sector mission.

---

## Model A: Service-Based Model (The "Red Hat" Approach)

### How It Works

Pioneered by Red Hat (acquired by IBM in 2019 for $34 billion), this model keeps the core product fully open and free while generating revenue through **professional services** layered on top. For the BOSC Community Library, this would mean:

- **Paid Support Tiers:** Organizations (schools, NGOs, government ministries) pay an annual fee for guaranteed response times, dedicated curators, and customized resource bundles.
- **Consulting Services:** Training workshops for educators on how to integrate BOSC resources into curricula, delivered for a fee.
- **Hosted Instances:** A managed, branded version of the library (with advanced search and analytics) offered as a subscription to institutions.
- **Certification Programs:** A paid "BOSC Certified Educator" credential verified by the library's maintainers.

### Strengths

- Revenue scales with adoption — as the library grows more popular, demand for premium services increases.
- The core library remains 100% free and open; no community alienation.
- Proven model: Red Hat sustained billion-dollar operations on this approach for over two decades.
- Incentivizes the core team to keep improving quality, since service value depends on underlying product quality.

### Weaknesses

- Requires a skilled, paid team to deliver professional services — difficult in the early stages.
- Revenue is tied to active sales efforts, not passive income.
- Governments may resist paying for services on something they expect to be "free."
- Scaling services globally (multiple languages, diverse legal contexts) is operationally complex.

## Model B: Foundation-Driven Model

### How It Works

Organizations like the **Mozilla Foundation**, **Wikimedia Foundation**, and **Linux Foundation** demonstrate that community assets can be sustainably funded through a non-profit foundation structure. For BOSC, this would mean:

- **Grant Funding:** Apply to international development grants (USAID, Gates Foundation, African Development Bank) aligned with digital education and open access.
- **Institutional Donations:** Solicit recurring donations from universities, government ministries, and international NGOs who benefit from the library.
- **Community Fundraising:** Annual donation campaigns to individual supporters (similar to Wikipedia's model).
- **Corporate Sponsorship:** Offer tiered sponsorship packages (Bronze/Silver/Gold) to technology companies wanting visibility in the African education sector.
- **In-Kind Contributions:** Solicit server hosting, design resources, and translation services from institutional partners in lieu of cash.

### Strengths

- Non-profit status increases trust and eligibility for public grants.
- Diverse funding sources reduce dependency on any single donor.
- Foundation governance creates formal accountability structures.
- Highly effective for globally recognized public goods (Wikimedia processes $150M+ annually this way).

### Weaknesses

- Non-profit registration and governance is legally complex and jurisdiction-specific.
- Grant funding is unpredictable — a project can be defunded when a grant cycle ends.
- Donation campaigns require significant marketing effort to build a donor base from scratch.
- Foundation overhead (legal, accounting, governance) can consume 15-25% of raised funds.

---

## Recommendation: A Hybrid Approach

Neither model is sufficient alone for an early-stage library in the East African context. We recommend a **phased hybrid strategy**:

**Phase 1 (Year 1-2): Foundation-First**  
Register as a community interest company or NGO. Pursue seed grants from UNESCO, the African Union's digital education initiatives, and technology company CSR programs. This establishes legal credibility and early financial runway without requiring a sales team.

**Phase 2 (Year 2-4): Service Layer Introduction**  
Once the library has 50+ institutional users, introduce paid support tiers and workshop offerings (Red Hat model). Revenue from services subsidizes core development and reduces grant dependency.

**Phase 3 (Year 4+): Self-Sustaining Operations**  
With a mature community and proven revenue model, reduce grant dependency to 30% of budget. Surplus from services funds new resource curation, localization, and governance operations.
